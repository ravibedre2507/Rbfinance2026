Section 1
Introduction to Accounting

SEO Title: What is Accounting? A Simple Guide for Beginners (2026)  |  Focus Keyword: what is accounting

1.1 What is Accounting?

Imagine you open a small tea stall outside your college. On Day 1, you spend ₹500 on milk, sugar, and tea leaves. You sell tea worth ₹800. At the end of the day, you have ₹300 extra in your pocket.

Now multiply this by 365 days. Can you remember every rupee that came in and went out? Of course not. That's why we need a system to record, organise, and understand money movement.

That system is called Accounting.

Simple Definition: Accounting is the language of business. It is the process of recording, classifying, summarising, and interpreting financial transactions so that anyone can clearly understand how a business is performing.

Think of accounting as Google Maps for your money. It tells you where your money came from, where it went, and where you stand right now.

1.2 A Short History of Accounting

Accounting is older than most countries.

  • 3000 BC — Ancient Mesopotamians recorded grain transactions on clay tablets.
  • 1494 AD — An Italian monk named Luca Pacioli wrote the first book on the double-entry system. He is called the Father of Accounting.
  • 1900s — Big firms emerged (Deloitte, PwC, EY, KPMG — the Big 4).
  • 2000s onwards — Accounting went digital with software like Tally, QuickBooks, Zoho Books, and SAP.
  • 2020s — AI and cloud accounting changed how we work forever.

Lesson: humans have been tracking money for over 5,000 years. You're not alone in finding it important.

1.3 Why Accounting is Important

  1. Know if you're making profit or loss — Without records, you're guessing.
  2. Pay correct tax — The government wants its share, and accounting tells you how much.
  3. Get loans — Banks check your books before lending.
  4. Attract investors — No investor puts money in a black box.
  5. Make smart decisions — Should you hire? Buy a machine? Cut costs?
  6. Detect fraud — Clean books expose dirty hands.
  7. Plan the future — Past numbers help predict tomorrow.

Section 2
Basic Accounting Concepts

SEO Title: Basic Accounting Terms Every Beginner Must Know  |  Focus Keyword: basic accounting concepts

Before learning rules, we need vocabulary. Imagine playing cricket without knowing what a "wicket" or "boundary" means. Same with accounting.

2.1 Assets

Assets are everything a business owns that has value and can bring future benefit.

Real-life example: You own a bike, mobile, savings in bank. These are your assets. For a shop — cash, stock of goods, furniture, machinery, building.

Types of Assets:

  • Fixed Assets — Long-term use (building, machinery, computer)
  • Current Assets — Used within 1 year (cash, stock, debtors)
  • Tangible Assets — Physical (car)
  • Intangible Assets — Invisible but valuable (brand name, patent, goodwill)

2.2 Liabilities

Liabilities are what a business owes to others.

Real-life example: A loan you took, EMI on your bike, salary you haven't paid yet.

2.3 Capital

Capital is the money or assets the owner invests in the business.

You take ₹50,000 from your savings and put it into your tea stall. That ₹50,000 is your capital.

Capital = Assets − Liabilities

2.4 Revenue (Income)

Revenue is the money a business earns from its main activities.

Money earned from selling tea = revenue. A YouTuber's ad income = revenue. A freelancer's project fee = revenue.

2.5 Expenses

Expenses are costs incurred to earn revenue.

Rent, electricity, salary, internet, transport — all expenses. No expense, no business. But too much expense, no profit.

2.6 Profit & Loss

Profit = Revenue − Expenses
Loss = Expenses − Revenue (when expenses are higher)

2.7 Goods

Goods are items a business buys to sell. Not for personal use.

For a bookshop, books are goods. The laptop the Apple employee uses is not goods — it's an asset.

2.8 Inventory (Stock)

Goods unsold and lying with the business at a given time. Opening Stock = stock at start of year. Closing Stock = stock at end of year.

2.9 Drawings

When the owner takes money/goods from the business for personal use. Reduces capital.

2.10 Equity

Owner's claim on the business after paying all liabilities. Equity = Capital + Retained Profits.

2.11 The Famous Accounting Equation

Assets = Liabilities + Capital

Whatever the business owns is funded either by outsiders (liabilities) or by the owner (capital). Always.

Example: Cash ₹50,000 + Stock ₹30,000 + Furniture ₹20,000 = ₹1,00,000 (assets). Loan ₹40,000 + Capital ₹60,000 = ₹1,00,000. ✓

Section 3
Accounting Principles (GAAP Basics)

SEO Title: Accounting Principles Simplified — Concepts Every Beginner Should Know  |  Focus Keyword: accounting principles for beginners

Accounting has rules — like cricket. Everyone follows them so the game is fair. These rules are called Accounting Principles or GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles).

3.1 Business Entity Concept

Business and owner are two separate beings.

Your father runs a sweet shop. If he takes ₹500 from the shop for home groceries, it's recorded as drawings — not a business expense.

3.2 Going Concern Concept

Assume the business will continue running for the foreseeable future.

A machine costing ₹10,00,000 lasting 10 years → we spread it (depreciate). We don't write off the entire ₹10 lakh today.

3.3 Money Measurement Concept

Only things measurable in money are recorded.

Employee loyalty is valuable but not in the books. Only what has clear money value goes in.

3.4 Accrual Concept

Income and expense are recorded when they happen — not when cash is received or paid.

You delivered a project in March, client paid in April. Under accrual, income is recorded in March.

3.5 Matching Principle

Expenses must be matched with the revenue they helped earn.

A bakery makes 1,000 cakes in March using flour bought in February. The flour is March's expense.

3.6 Conservatism (Prudence)

Record all expected losses, but never expected profits.

Court case may cost you ₹50,000? Record it. May win ₹10 lakh tender? Don't record until it happens.

3.7 Consistency Principle

Once you choose a method, stick to it.

If you use Straight Line Depreciation this year, use it next year too. Otherwise comparisons become useless.

3.8 Materiality Principle

Record what matters. Ignore what's trivial.

A ₹100 pen — don't depreciate over 5 years. Just expense it.

3.9 Full Disclosure

Tell the user everything important. Hide nothing.

3.10 Realisation Concept

Income is recognised only when it's earned — not when an order is placed.

Section 4
The Golden Rules of Accounting

SEO Title: Golden Rules of Accounting Explained Simply with Examples  |  Focus Keyword: golden rules of accounting

This is the heart of accounting. If you understand this, half your work is done.

4.1 What is an Account?

A record of money movement related to a specific person, thing, or expense. Think of it as a folder. Examples: Cash Account, Salary Account, Rent Account, Ravi's Account.

4.2 The 3 Types of Accounts

TypeTracksExample
PersonalPersons, firms, companiesRavi A/c, ICICI Bank A/c, ABC Ltd. A/c
RealPhysical things, assetsCash A/c, Furniture A/c, Building A/c
NominalExpenses, incomes, gains, lossesSalary A/c, Rent A/c, Commission A/c

4.3 The 3 Golden Rules

Rule 1 — Personal Account: Debit the Receiver, Credit the Giver
Rule 2 — Real Account: Debit what comes in, Credit what goes out
Rule 3 — Nominal Account: Debit all expenses and losses, Credit all incomes and gains

4.4 The Modern Approach (American Approach)

ElementIncreaseDecrease
AssetsDebitCredit
LiabilitiesCreditDebit
CapitalCreditDebit
IncomeCreditDebit
ExpensesDebitCredit

4.5 Memory Trick — DEAD CLIC

DEBIT = Expenses, Assets, Drawings
CREDIT = Liabilities, Income, Capital

If you memorise this one line, you can pass entries for a lifetime.

4.6 Worked Examples

Example 1: Started business with ₹50,000 cash.
Cash A/c Dr. ₹50,000 / To Capital A/c ₹50,000

Example 2: Paid Rent ₹5,000.
Rent A/c Dr. ₹5,000 / To Cash A/c ₹5,000

Example 3: Sold goods for ₹10,000 cash.
Cash A/c Dr. ₹10,000 / To Sales A/c ₹10,000

4.7 Common Mistakes

  1. Confusing personal name with cash.
  2. Treating salary as personal — it's nominal.
  3. Forgetting capital is credited when owner brings money in.

5.1 What is a Journal?

A Journal is the first book of accounts where every transaction is recorded in chronological order — day by day. Also called the Book of Original Entry.

Think of it as your daily diary of business transactions.

5.3 Steps to Pass a Journal Entry

  1. Identify the two accounts involved.
  2. Classify each (Personal / Real / Nominal).
  3. Apply golden rules or DEAD CLIC.
  4. Decide Debit vs Credit.
  5. Write the entry with date and narration.

5.5 Common Errors in Journal Entries

  1. Writing "To" before the debit line.
  2. Not writing narration.
  3. Confusing trade discount (no entry) with cash discount (entry required).
  4. Putting GST in wrong account.
  5. Not splitting amounts when multiple accounts are involved.

Section 6
Ledger Accounts

SEO Title: Ledger Accounts Explained — T-Format with Real Examples  |  Focus Keyword: ledger posting

6.1 What is a Ledger?

A Ledger is the principal book where transactions from the journal are grouped account-wise.

If a journal is a daily diary, the ledger is a personal file for each account. All entries related to Rent go into "Rent A/c" ledger, no matter what date.

6.2 T-Format

            Cash A/c
-------------------------------------
   Dr.                          Cr.
-------------------------------------
Date  Particulars  Amt | Date  Particulars  Amt
01/04 To Capital  50000 | 02/04 By Rent      3000
05/04 To Sales    12000 | 03/04 By Salary   10000
                        | 31/04 By Balance c/d 49000
                  62000 |                    62000
  • Left side = Debit (Dr.)
  • Right side = Credit (Cr.)
  • Debit entries: To ...
  • Credit entries: By ...

6.3 Steps to Post from Journal to Ledger

  1. Pick a journal entry.
  2. Identify the debited account → write on Dr. side: "To [Credit account] [amount]"
  3. Identify the credited account → write on Cr. side: "By [Debit account] [amount]"

6.4 Balancing a Ledger

At month-end, total both sides. The difference = balance.

  • Debit total > Credit total → Debit balance (Asset/Expense)
  • Credit total > Debit total → Credit balance (Liability/Income)

Trial Balance

7.1 Meaning

A Trial Balance is a list of all ledger balances on a given date. If done correctly, debit total = credit total.

7.3 What Goes on Which Side?

SideItems
DebitAssets, Expenses, Drawings, Purchases, Sales Returns, Discount Allowed, Bad Debts
CreditLiabilities, Capital, Income, Sales, Purchase Returns, Discount Received, Reserves

7.4 Errors NOT Disclosed by Trial Balance

  1. Error of Omission — Entry not recorded.
  2. Error of Commission — Wrong amount on both sides.
  3. Error of Principle — Capital expense treated as revenue.
  4. Compensating Errors — Two opposite errors cancel out.

7.5 Suspense Account

When trial balance doesn't agree, the difference is parked in a Suspense Account temporarily. When error is found, suspense is cleared.

Section 9
Depreciation

SEO Title: Depreciation Explained — SLM vs WDV Methods with Examples  |  Focus Keyword: depreciation methods

9.1 Meaning

Depreciation is the gradual decrease in the value of a fixed asset due to use, wear & tear, or passage of time.

You buy a bike for ₹80,000. After 5 years it's worth ₹30,000. The ₹50,000 difference is depreciation.

9.2 Why Depreciation Matters

  1. To show true value of asset in balance sheet.
  2. To match cost with revenue (matching principle).
  3. To provide funds for replacement.
  4. To calculate correct profit.
  5. To comply with law and tax rules.

9.3 Straight Line Method (SLM)

Same amount of depreciation each year.

Depreciation = (Cost − Scrap Value) ÷ Useful Life

Machine ₹50,000, scrap ₹5,000, life 5 years → Depreciation = (50,000 − 5,000) / 5 = ₹9,000/year

10.4 Input Tax Credit (ITC)

If you paid GST while buying, you can deduct it from the GST you collect while selling.

Buy ₹50,000 + GST ₹9,000 → ITC ₹9,000.
Sell ₹80,000 + GST ₹14,400.
Net GST to pay = ₹14,400 − ₹9,000 = ₹5,400

Section 11
Cash Flow Statement

SEO Title: Cash Flow Statement Explained — Operating, Investing, Financing  |  Focus Keyword: cash flow statement

11.1 What is a Cash Flow Statement?

A report showing how cash moved in and out of a business during a period.

Profit on paper ≠ Cash in pocket. A business can be profitable but still bankrupt if cash runs out.

11.2 Three Sections

A. Operating Activities — Day-to-day cash flow. Inflows: customer payments. Outflows: suppliers, salary, rent, taxes.

B. Investing Activities — Buying/selling long-term assets. Inflows: sale of machinery. Outflows: purchase of land.

C. Financing Activities — Money from owners or lenders. Inflows: loans, capital. Outflows: loan repayment, dividend.

Section 12
Financial Ratio Analysis

SEO Title: Financial Ratios Explained — Liquidity, Profitability, Solvency  |  Focus Keyword: ratio analysis

12.1 Liquidity Ratios

Current Ratio = Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities (Ideal 2:1)
Quick Ratio = (CA − Stock) ÷ CL (Ideal 1:1)

12.2 Profitability Ratios

Gross Profit % = (GP ÷ Sales) × 100
Net Profit % = (NP ÷ Sales) × 100
ROCE = (NP ÷ Capital Employed) × 100

12.3 Solvency Ratios

Debt-Equity Ratio = Debt ÷ Equity (Ideal ≤ 2)
Interest Coverage = EBIT ÷ Interest (Ideal ≥ 4)

12.4 Activity (Turnover) Ratios

Inventory Turnover = COGS ÷ Avg Stock
Debtors Turnover = Credit Sales ÷ Avg Debtors

Section 14
Practical Business Accounting

SEO Title: How to Do Accounting for a Shop, Freelancer, or Startup  |  Focus Keyword: small business accounting

14.1 Shop Accounting (Retail)

  1. Note opening cash.
  2. Record every sale (cash & UPI separately).
  3. Record purchases.
  4. Note expenses.
  5. Closing cash = Opening + Sales − Expenses − Drawings.
  6. Match physical cash with book.

14.2 Freelancer Accounting

Track: invoices raised, payments received, TDS deducted, expenses, GST (if turnover > ₹20 lakh). Use Zoho Invoice, Refrens, or Excel.

14.3 Startup Accounting

Founder's capital, funding records, cap table, monthly burn rate, runway, investor reports. Hire a CA from Day 1.

14.4 Small Business Bookkeeping Checklist

Daily: Record cash sales, expenses, save bills.
Weekly: Reconcile bank, update debtor/creditor list.
Monthly: P&L, pay salaries, file GST, take backup.
Yearly: Final accounts, file ITR, audit.

Advanced Accounting Basics

SEO Title: Advanced Accounting Concepts — Accruals, Provisions, Bad Debts  |  Focus Keyword: advanced accounting concepts

15.1 Accrual vs Cash Accounting

Cash BasisAccrual Basis
Record when cash movesRecord when transaction happens
SimpleMore accurate
For freelancers, small bizFor companies, audit-ready

15.2 Provisions vs Reserves

Provision = expected loss (e.g., Provision for Doubtful Debts).
Reserve = expected strength out of profit (General Reserve, Capital Reserve).

15.3 Bad Debts

Customer doesn't pay. Entry: Bad Debts A/c Dr. / To Customer A/c. Charged to P&L.

15.4 Outstanding Expenses

Incurred but unpaid. Example: rent due for March, unpaid.
Entry: Rent A/c Dr. / To Outstanding Rent A/c

15.5 Prepaid Expenses

Paid in advance for next period. Example: annual insurance paid in January.
Entry: Prepaid Insurance A/c Dr. / To Insurance A/c

15.6 Accrued Income & Income in Advance

Accrued = earned but not received. Income in advance = received but not yet earned.

Section 16
Common Accounting Mistakes

SEO Title: 20 Common Accounting Mistakes Beginners Make  |  Focus Keyword: accounting mistakes

16.1 Beginner Errors (Top 10)

  1. Mixing personal and business money — biggest sin.
  2. No backup of data — one crash, everything gone.
  3. Forgetting GST input claim — money left on table.
  4. Confusing purchase with asset.
  5. Ignoring small expenses — ₹100/day = ₹36,500/year.
  6. Not recording cash sales.
  7. No invoice numbering — chaos at year-end.
  8. Wrong GST rate applied — penalty risk.
  9. Treating drawings as expense.
  10. No depreciation booked.

16.2 Fraud Examples

  • Ghost employees in payroll.
  • Fake invoices to inflate purchases.
  • Skimming — pocketing some cash sales.
  • Over-billing related parties.

16.3 Tax Mistakes

  • Late GST filing → late fee + interest.
  • Wrong HSN code → notice.
  • Claiming personal expenses as business → disallowed.
  • Forgetting TDS → 1% interest per month.

16.4 Prevention Checklist

  • Open separate bank account for business.
  • Use software, not paper.
  • Reconcile bank monthly.
  • Save digital + physical copies.
  • Consult a CA at least once a year.

Accounting Interview Questions

SEO Title: Top 30 Accounting Interview Questions with Sample Answers  |  Focus Keyword: accounting interview questions

17.1 Beginner Q&A

Q1. What is accounting?
Recording, classifying, summarising and interpreting financial transactions.

Q2. Difference between accounting and bookkeeping?
Bookkeeping is just recording; accounting includes analysis and reporting.

Q3. What are the golden rules?
Personal: Debit receiver, credit giver. Real: Debit what comes in, credit what goes out. Nominal: Debit expenses, credit incomes.

Q4. What is the accounting equation?
Assets = Liabilities + Capital.

Q5. Define journal and ledger.
Journal = chronological book of original entry. Ledger = grouped account-wise.

17.2 Practical Questions

Q6. Why is depreciation charged? — To spread cost over useful life.
Q7. Difference between provision and reserve? — Provision = loss. Reserve = strength.
Q8. What is matching principle? — Match expense with revenue.
Q9. What is contingent liability? — Possible liability dependent on a future event.
Q10. Trial balance vs balance sheet? — TB = list of balances. BS = financial position on a date.

17.3 MCQs

  1. Which is a real account? (a) Salary (b) Capital (c) Furniture (d) Bank Loan — Answer: c
  2. Outstanding salary is: (a) Asset (b) Liability (c) Income (d) Expense — Answer: b
  3. Book of original entry: (a) Ledger (b) Trial balance (c) Journal (d) Balance sheet — Answer: c
  4. Depreciation is: (a) Cash expense (b) Non-cash expense (c) Income (d) Asset — Answer: b
  5. Capital Account is: (a) Real (b) Nominal (c) Personal (d) None — Answer: c

17.4 Scenario-Based
